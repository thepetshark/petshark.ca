Beamed down from Hometown

This study asks how a beam could come down from the sky, off camera, when the player places a worker or a trap on the map, as if it were being beamed down from the player's floating Hometown above. Here are six ways it could look if Unity's effects were used, playing on the game's real map. Drag the bar under any phone to move through its effect by hand.

Concepts only. These are browser simulations, not Unity captures. Each is made only of the kinds of parts Unity's effect tools provide, drawn through a camera set to the game's own numbers, so a vertical beam leans, widens and foreshortens at each zoom as it would in the game. Today a placement has no effect at all: the pin appears and a notice says "Trap placed".

What building this showed

The zoom changes a beam more than its design doesZoomed in, a beam stands against the sky. At the default zoom it leans away from the middle of the screen as it rises. Zoomed out you look down along it: it fans toward the top edge and is almost three times wider there than at the ground. Try the zoom switch on any of them.
By day, added light disappears on bright grassA purely additive beam is nearly invisible at noon on the meadow. Every beam here also has a pale body drawn with ordinary blending by day. In Unity that is one premultiplied-alpha material instead of an additive one.
What arrives is a pinThe game has no model of a trap or a worker on the map, only a pin, so that is what these deliver. "Lowered" and "Print" would gain the most from a small model: there would be something real to lower, or to build from the ground up.
It runs under the interfaceThe beam is in the world and the interface is on top, so it passes behind the coin and water counters. Today's "Trap placed" notice would cover its top hundred pixels.
It will be seen twenty times in a rowA player setting out traps sees this again and again, so it should be short and never block the next tap. "Drop" is done in a second. The two-second ones suit a first placement better than the twentieth.
The cost is the tall see-through surfaceWhat a phone pays for is large transparent areas drawn over each other, and a column that fills the screen from top to bottom when zoomed in is exactly that. "Drop", "Print" and "Ribbons" have almost none.
